# Traditional care for smoky air

### What Ayurveda offers people living with Bali's dry-season air, sorted by how much we can stand behind it

> **Status: draft, not yet on the public web app.** This needs a read by a qualified Ayurvedic practitioner and a medical doctor practising in Bali before any of it is shown on the site. Until then it lives here, where it can be corrected in the open.

---

## Read this first

The first thing Ayurveda prescribes for any illness is *nidana parivarjana*: remove the cause. For smoky air that means the same thing the community already worked out and measured. Wear a well-fitted N95 or KN95 on the motorbike, seal the room before you filter it, run the purifier in the room you are actually in, and report the fire. Those steps lower the dose of PM2.5 reaching your lungs. Nothing on this page does that.

What this page covers is the second layer: caring for a throat, nose and chest that have already taken a day of smoke and dust. Some of it has clinical evidence behind it, some of it is traditional practice that is safe but unproven, and some of it is risky for children. We say which is which, the same way [community knowledge](community-knowledge.md) keeps *tested*, *reported* and *contested* apart.

- **Supported** — a traditional practice that also has clinical evidence behind it, independent of the tradition.
- **Traditional** — Ayurvedic practice, low risk for healthy adults, without good modern evidence. Offered as care, not as treatment.
- **Caution** — can cause harm for some people. Stated with the limit that matters.
- **Not published** — claims in the source we will not repeat, and why.

**This is not medical advice.** A cough that lasts more than three weeks, a fever, wheezing, chest pain, coughing blood, or any breathing difficulty in a baby or small child needs a doctor, not a tea.

## Where this comes from

The source is a household guide written for one household during the 2026 dry season, drawing on Ayurvedic practice and on plants that grow in Balinese gardens. It was written for particular people, not as a reference text, so we have removed everything personal, dropped claims we could not stand behind, and added the safety limits a public page needs. It has not yet been reviewed by a practitioner; that is the next step.

## 1. After a smoky day · rinse, gargle, hydrate

These are the practices worth doing on any evening after hours on the road or near a fire.

**Saline nasal rinse** (*jala neti*) · **Supported, with one hard rule.** Rinsing the nose with salt water washes out trapped dust and soot before it drains into the throat overnight, and saline irrigation has good clinical evidence for relieving nose and sinus symptoms. Adults can use a neti pot or a squeeze bottle; for children, use a ready-made saline spray. **The rule: never rinse with tap water.** Use boiled-and-cooled, distilled or sterile water, every time. Unboiled water can carry amoebae that are harmless to drink but can be fatal in the nose, and Bali's tap water is not safe to drink in the first place.

**Warm salt gargle** · **Supported.** Half a glass of warm water with a quarter teaspoon of fine salt, gargled for thirty seconds and spat out, two or three times a day. It loosens thick mucus and soothes a coated throat. A pinch of turmeric is traditional; it does no harm, but the salt and warm water are doing the work.

**Warm fluids through the day** · **Traditional, and sensible.** Dry, dusty air dries out the lining of the nose and throat, and a dry lining is an irritable one. Keep a warm pot going and sip from it. Local plants make good bases:

- *Daun pandan* (pandan) with *serai* (lemongrass) and one crushed *kapulaga* (cardamom) pod — the household favourite, gentle enough for the whole family.
- *Daun mint* — cooling, and children usually like it.
- *Ketumbar* (coriander seed), half a teaspoon lightly crushed, with a pinch of fennel.
- Tulsi, a slice of ginger and one whole clove — warming and stronger, better for adults.

Ayurveda sorts these into *warming* (tulsi, ginger, clove) and *cooling* (pandan, mint, coriander, fennel). If warming herbs bring on a rash, itchy skin or a hot, irritable feeling, the tradition's advice is to switch to the cooling ones.

**Skip ice** · **Traditional.** Ayurveda holds that very cold drinks and fridge-cold food slow digestion and thicken mucus. There is no good evidence either way, and warm drinks are kinder to a sore throat.

## 2. For a raw, coated throat

**Raw honey** · **Supported for adults and children over one year. Never for babies under twelve months.** Honey eases coughs, and it is one of the few home remedies with decent trial evidence in children. Ayurveda calls its action *lekhana*, scraping, and says to stir it into drinks that have cooled to body temperature, not boiling water. **Honey can carry botulism spores that are dangerous to infants: no honey, in any form, before the first birthday.**

**Licorice root** (*yashtimadhu*) · **Caution.** Traditionally the herb for the throat: a pinch of powder held on the tongue soothes raw tissue. But licorice raises blood pressure and lowers potassium when taken regularly. Keep it to short courses of a few days, avoid it during pregnancy and with high blood pressure, heart or kidney conditions, and do not give it to children without a practitioner.

**Propolis** · **Traditional.** A drop is used to coat a raw throat. Avoid it if you react to bee stings or pollen.

**Fresh pineapple** · **Traditional.** A few spoonfuls of fresh, room-temperature pineapple juice between meals. Pineapple contains bromelain, an enzyme that breaks down proteins; whether the amount in a few spoonfuls of juice thins mucus is unproven. Harmless for most people.

**Warm milk or water with a little ghee before bed** · **Traditional.** A demulcent: it coats the throat. If astringent herbal teas are leaving your throat drier, the tradition suggests switching to this.

## 3. The oil barrier (*nasya*) · Caution

Ayurveda recommends a tiny amount of warm ghee or sesame oil just inside the nostrils before going out, as a barrier that catches dust. It is a widely taught daily practice, and a few studies of nasal barrier balms suggest they can reduce symptoms from pollen. **We could find no evidence that it reduces the PM2.5 reaching the lungs**, and a mask does that far better.

It also carries a real risk. Oil drawn deep into the nose can reach the lungs and cause lipoid pneumonia, a documented injury in babies whose parents put oil in their noses, and in adults who use oil nasally at bedtime while lying down. So:

- **Adults only**, a thin smear at the entrance of the nostril, not a coating.
- **Plant oil or ghee only**, never mineral oil or petroleum products.
- **Not at bedtime.**
- **Never for babies or small children.**

The source also suggests warm sesame or coconut oil with a pinch of salt rubbed on a child's chest and back before sleep. That is a gentle massage, safe on the skin, and many families find it settles a child. It does not reach the lungs, and it is not a treatment.

## 4. Timing the windows

The source says to keep doors and windows closed between 5:00 and 8:00 in the morning and evening, when smoke hangs lowest. That matches what the community observed about dawn and dusk burning, and what the sensors show on most days. But the network can do better than a fixed rule: the hourly readings near you show when *your* air is actually worst, and that is what the web app should use.

## 5. Children · the limits in one place

Everything in this box is a hard limit, not a preference.

- **No honey before twelve months.**
- **No oil in a baby's or small child's nose.**
- **No clove oil or other essential oils by mouth for children.** A whole clove steeped in an adult's tea is fine; concentrated clove oil can poison a small child.
- **No licorice for children** without a practitioner.
- **Saline for noses: a ready-made spray, never home-mixed tap water.**
- Warm drinks, frequent small sips of water, and gentle foods such as stewed pears are safe and soothing.
- **See a doctor** for fast or laboured breathing, the skin pulling in between the ribs, a fever in a baby under three months, a cough lasting more than three weeks, or a child who is unusually drowsy or not feeding.

Children breathe more air for their size than adults do, so the first thing to change for them is the dose: a well-fitted child mask, a sealed room with a purifier, and play moved away from smoky hours.

## 6. Not published

- **"Tulsi filters carbon and exhaust particles out of the blood."** There is no such mechanism. Inhaled fine particles are not filtered out by any herb. Tulsi tea is a pleasant warming drink; that is all we will say for it.
- **"A damp towel in the bedroom weighs down airborne dust."** There is no evidence it lowers PM2.5 measurably, and in the tropics it adds humidity and mould. This is exactly the kind of claim a member with an indoor sensor could test overnight. If someone does, the result goes into community knowledge whichever way it lands.
- **"Green phlegm means your immune system is cleaning house."** Partly true: the colour comes from white blood cells. But colour alone tells you nothing about whether you need a doctor. Duration, fever and breathing do.
- **"Turmeric is antibacterial."** Only in laboratory dishes. It is harmless in a gargle; it is not an antibiotic.
- **Personal protocols.** The source includes advice tailored to named individuals, including strong herbal formulas. Formulas like that belong in a consultation with a practitioner, not on a public page.

## 7. What would make this better

**A reviewer.** An Ayurvedic practitioner (BAMS or equivalent) and a doctor in Bali, named in this document with their permission, reading it line by line.

**Bali's own tradition.** Ayurveda came to Bali from outside. Balinese *usada* and Indonesian *jamu* already have daily drinks, the *loloh*, and household plants for coughs and throats that many residents learned from their grandmothers. A version of this page that starts from what a Balinese household already knows, and asks a *balian usada* or jamu makers what they use in the smoky months, would reach far more of the people breathing this air than an imported vocabulary will.

**Your practice.** If you have something that works for you in the dry season, tell the WhatsApp community. Reports come into [community knowledge](community-knowledge.md) as *reported*, anonymous, and get tested where they can be.
